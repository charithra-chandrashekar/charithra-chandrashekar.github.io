SimXGNN: Master's Thesis

04 · full deep-dive
PES University · Published as an EAI Endorsed Transactions preprint

Similarity-Enhanced Cross-layered Graph Neural Network for drug–target interaction prediction

Predicting how a drug molecule will interact with a biological target is central to drug discovery, but traditional wet-lab screening is slow and expensive, and most computational methods encode drugs and targets separately, missing the substructure relationships that actually drive an interaction. SimXGNN tackles this by learning drug and protein representations jointly, interleaving three graph convolution styles (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE) and training with drug–protein similarity signals alongside contrastive learning, so the model captures both fine-grained local chemistry and broader structural context at once.

Code on GitHub →

Dataset: a fused, heterogeneous drug–target graph

Rather than relying on one data source, the training graph was assembled from four separate biological databases, each contributing a different layer of information: interaction networks, chemical structure, protein sequence, and, where no experimental structure existed, AlphaFold-predicted protein folds. The result is a single heterogeneous graph of 12,015 nodes spanning drugs, proteins, diseases, and side effects, connected through six categories of similarity and interaction edges.

708 drugs (DrugBank)
1,512 proteins (UniProt / PDB / AlphaFold)
5,603 diseases
4,192 side effects

Architecture: two interleaved GNN variants

Drug molecules and protein sequences are each first converted into their own graphs: atoms and bonds for drugs, amino-acid residues and their contacts for proteins, and passed through a GCN feature-extraction stage to produce initial embeddings. Those embeddings then feed into one of two proposed hybrid models, each combining a different pair of convolution styles in an alternating pattern, trained jointly with a collaborative contrastive loss and an adaptive self-paced sampling strategy that prioritises the most informative negative examples as training progresses.

SimXGNN–GS: alternating GAT to SAGE layers
SimXGNN–GC: alternating GAT to GCN layers
  • SimXGNN–GS (GAT–SAGE): GAT layers dynamically weight task-relevant neighbours first, then SAGE layers aggregate efficiently across broader neighbourhoods, favouring scalability on large graphs.
  • SimXGNN–GC (GAT–GCN): GAT again identifies important local neighbours first, then GCN applies uniform structural smoothing, reducing noise while preserving global consistency. This variant performed best overall.

End-to-end workflow

Drug and protein graphs are embedded independently, fused with their respective similarity networks, combined into a joint representation, and scored by an MLP decoder to predict interaction probability, with the whole pipeline trained under the collaborative contrastive objective described above.

Simplified data flow, drug embedding → prediction

Results: benchmarked against 5 baselines

Both SimXGNN variants were evaluated against traditional GNN baselines (GCN, GAT), a strong prior drug-target model (GraphDTA), a contrastive baseline (MSGCL), and the most recent state-of-the-art method, CCL-ASPS, across five standard metrics. SimXGNN–GC came out on top on every metric measured.

Against CCL-ASPS, the previous best-performing method, SimXGNN–GC improved AUROC by 1.92% and AUPR by 1.37%, while also posting the highest accuracy, F1, and MCC scores of any method tested, indicating gains not just in raw predictive power but in overall classification reliability and robustness to the dataset's class imbalance.

Key takeaways

  • Interleaving attention-based (GAT) and structural (GCN/SAGE) convolutions in sequence, rather than stacking one style repeatedly, let the model capture both fine-grained local chemistry and broader graph structure.
  • Fusing drug–protein similarity matrices directly into training, on top of the interaction graph itself, gave the model an extra signal that plain interaction data alone doesn't provide.
  • Adaptive self-paced negative sampling meant the hardest, most informative negative examples were prioritised as training progressed, rather than sampling uniformly at random.
  • The GAT–GCN ordering outperformed GAT–SAGE in this setting, suggesting that after attention-based local refinement, uniform structural smoothing generalised better than sampled aggregation for this dataset.
